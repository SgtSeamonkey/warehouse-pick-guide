PHONE-FRIENDLY TEXT VOTER GUIDE WITH AD-MIX AND PROPOSAL RISKS

IMPORTANT NOTE ON ADS AND SPENDING
- Exact ad spending by category is not consistently published for every candidate and every race.
- The campaign ad market is often opaque, especially for local, state, and ballot-line races.
- This guide therefore uses a BEST-EFFORT campaign-ad profile based on typical partisan strategy, public ad patterns, and public reporting, not a definitive audited count.
- Where exact number/percentage data is not known, it is marked: "No reliable public ad-spend breakdown found."
- AD TYPES:
  * Positive / issue-focused ads: candidate talks about their own platform and qualifications.
  * Attack / negative ads: candidate attacks an opponent.
  * Fear-based attack ads: use crime, chaos, loss of rights, inflation, etc.
  * Voting-history attack ads: highlight opponent's past votes or prior positions.
  * Mixed ads: both positive and negative messaging.

IMPORTANT NOTE ON PROPOSALS
- For ballot proposals, the key question is not simply "Do I want to save money?"
- The main issues are: what the proposal changes, who it impacts, what it costs or saves, and what unexpected outcomes it may create.
- Many proposals can sound simple but create legal, administrative, or policy effects that are broader than they first appear.
- If a proposal's downside is only "save money," that is usually a weak or incomplete argument.
- Downside concerns can include: tax changes, broadened legal obligations, hidden administrative burden, loss of local control, speech restrictions, or unintended policy consequences.

GENERAL GUIDANCE
- Check the final official ballot and county clerk sheet before voting.
- For ad spending and ad frequency, use campaign finance reports, TV/online buys, and independent trackers like AdImpact or state campaign disclosure data.
- For judicial and local races, campaign ad tactics are often less predictable and less partisan than statewide partisan contests.

PROPOSALS AND AUTHORITY MILLAGES
------------------------------------------------------------------

PROPOSAL 2026-1
A proposal to convene a constitutional convention to draft a general revision of the state constitution.
Ballot question:
Shall a convention of elected delegates be convened in 2027 to draft a general revision of the State Constitution for presentation to the state’s voters for their approval or rejection?

Pros / arguments for yes:
- Allows major constitutional reform if voters think the state constitution is outdated.
- May address long-standing structural issues.
- Gives voters a direct chance to approve or reject a full revision.

Possible concerns / counterpoints:
- This is not just a cost-saving proposal. A constitutional convention can lead to unexpected, broad, and difficult-to-control results.
- A broad rewrite can trigger many issues beyond the original complaint: election rules, taxation, rights, voting laws, local power, and government structure.
- It could be expensive and create long, contentious legal and political fights.
- Downsides are not limited to money; the real risk is that the convention may produce changes no one expected.
- Supporters of the status quo argue it may create uncertainty and overreach.

PROPOSAL 2026-2
A proposed initiated law to prohibit campaign contributions from certain regulated utilities and government contractors and expand campaign finance disclosure rules.
Ballot question:
Should this proposal be adopted?

Pros / arguments for yes:
- Reduces the influence of regulated utilities and large contractors in politics.
- Expands transparency for campaign-related communications.
- Makes digital political communications more visible to the public.

Possible concerns / counterpoints:
- This is not only a "save money" issue. The real concern is whether the law is drafted too broadly or too vaguely.
- Some critics argue it may chill speech, create legal confusion, or broaden disclosure rules beyond reasonable limits.
- It may create unintended compliance burdens for campaigns, media, and political groups.
- There is risk of litigation over definitions, enforcement, and what counts as a political communication.
- A supporter of the idea might argue it is anti-corruption, while a critic may say it expands state power in a way that could be expensive or legally complicated.

OAKLAND COUNTY ZOOLOGICAL AUTHORITY MILLAGE
Ballot question:
Do you approve the renewal and restoration of the levy of 0.10 mill on all taxable property in Oakland County for a period of ten years, from 2028 through 2037?

Pros / arguments for yes:
- Supports animal care, educational programming, and facility maintenance at the Detroit Zoo.
- Continues long-running public cultural and educational services.
- Keeps a millage rate unchanged from previous voter approvals.

Possible concerns / counterpoints:
- This is not simply a "save money" or "don’t pay attention" issue. It is a tax extension that raises ongoing county costs.
- Critics may argue the county should evaluate whether services are truly essential or whether some savings are possible.
- The real downside is not a hidden surprise so much as the long-term property tax commitment and ongoing stewardship question.
- Supporters point to public benefit; opponents may focus on tax burden and whether a zoo millage is the best use of county funds.

SOUTHEAST MICHIGAN PUBLIC HISTORICAL MUSEUM AUTHORITY MILLAGE
Ballot question:
Shall a 0.2 mill tax on all taxable property located within Oakland County be imposed by the Authority for a period of ten years, being years 2026 through 2035, inclusive, to provide historical museum services for the benefit of the residents of the Counties?

Pros / arguments for yes:
- Supports historical museums, educational programming, and public cultural institutions.
- Provides long-term funding for museums that serve residents and visitors.
- Helps maintain public access to historical, educational, and cultural institutions.

Possible concerns / counterpoints:
- This is not just a "save money" issue; it is a new ongoing tax tied to museum funding.
- A downside is that it may impose a larger tax burden on property owners for a service some residents do not directly use.
- Critics may question whether county funding should prioritize museums versus schools, roads, public safety, or other core services.
- Supporters emphasize community and educational values; opponents focus on the cost and whether the tax is justified.

AD SPEND AND NEGATIVE-AD NOTES: QUICK TAKE
------------------------------------------------------------------
- In major statewide races, attack ads are often more common than in local school board or judicial races.
- Fear-based attack ads are more common in statewide partisan contests on Governor, Senate, and high-profile congressional races.
- Voting-history attacks usually appear in races where opponents have a clear difference in voting record or party identity.
- In local judicial and school board races, the ad mix is often more positive and less fear-driven.
- Many smaller-party and local races do not have reliable public ad-spend data by category, so exact percentages are usually not available.
- A candidate may run mostly positive ads even if they have some attack spots; the campaign ad mix is often not uniform across a whole race.
